The gap this project exists to close is not a gap in data. NEPSE publishes a great deal — daily bars for every listed company, company disclosures, and a broker-level floorsheet showing who traded with whom, which many larger exchanges do not release publicly at all.

The gap is in processing. Almost none of that data reaches an ordinary investor in a form they can use. It arrives as files nobody can read at scale, spread across sources, in formats that change over time, with no way to ask the obvious comparative question: is what I am looking at unusual for this stock, or is it Tuesday?

The vision is therefore narrower and more stubborn than "AI will predict the market". It is to turn public data that already exists into measurements an ordinary investor can act on, and to be relentlessly honest about the limits of what those measurements can tell anyone.

## What "smarter" actually means here

Three things, and none of them is prediction.

**Measurement.** Replacing impressions with numbers that have definitions. "Volume looks high" becomes "turnover is in the top few per cent of this stock's own sessions over the past year". The second statement can be checked, argued with and tested against history. The first cannot.

**Memory.** A person compares today against what they remember, and memory is reconstructed rather than stored. An archive compares today against every session that actually happened. Most of the value in market data is comparative, and comparison is exactly what human recall is worst at.

**Honesty.** Every number states which session it came from, whether it is live or historical, and how confident anyone should be in it. This sounds like a detail. It is the whole product. A stale figure presented as current is the most common failure in market software anywhere, and it is worse than showing nothing, because it invites confidence it has not earned.

> The aim is not a machine that tells you what to buy. It is a machine that guarantees you actually looked at everything before you decided.

## The principles this is built against

These are constraints, not features. They exist because each one prevents a specific way of misleading people.

1. **Describe before predicting.** "This broker took forty per cent of today's volume" is a fact. "Therefore the price will rise" is a claim requiring evidence, and the two must never appear in the same typeface.
2. **Every number carries its session.** During market hours, live means live and history is labelled as history. A price whose own timestamp is not from the current session is refused rather than displayed, because a feed will happily serve yesterday's last trade long after the close.
3. **Publish what failed.** A research process whose output is always positive is not measuring anything. Ideas that die in testing are more informative than the ones that survive, and hiding them is how a platform becomes marketing.
4. **Normalise against the instrument.** A market containing large banks and small hydropower companies has no common scale. Every threshold is relative to the stock's own history.
5. **Refuse look-ahead.** Any figure attached to a date must be computable from data available on or before that date. This is boring, expensive, and the difference between research and a flattering illusion.
6. **Show uncertainty.** A system that always has a confident answer is not measuring confidence. Where the honest answer is "we do not know", that is what should appear.

## What this deliberately is not

Being clear about what a project refuses to be is more useful than another list of features.

- **Not a signal service.** No buy calls, no target prices, no "sure shot" lists. Those exist in abundance already and they are the single largest source of retail loss.
- **Not a promise of returns.** Nobody can make that promise honestly, and every historical record you have seen quoted was selected after the fact.
- **Not a black box.** A score you cannot decompose is a number you have to take on faith, and faith is not a research method. Every published figure should be explainable in a sentence you can verify by hand.
- **Not a hype feed.** Attention and returns are different objectives, and optimising for the first reliably damages the second.

## The floorsheet deserves to be treated as public infrastructure

NEPSE's broker-level trade data is the most distinctive asset an ordinary Nepali investor has. It is granular in a way that most retail investors elsewhere in the world simply do not get. And it is almost entirely unused, because reading it by hand is impossible and nobody has done the unglamorous work of turning it into something answerable.

Processed properly, it supports questions that no traditional workflow can pose. Was today's volume concentrated in a few brokers or spread across many? Has participation broadened as the price moved, or narrowed? How does one broker's net position over the past week compare with its own normal behaviour in that stock?

It also punishes the naive, and that punishment is instructive. Every share bought is sold, so any net buy or sell imbalance computed across the whole market is exactly zero by construction. Many exciting-sounding floorsheet metrics are re-descriptions of turnover wearing a disguise, and they vanish the moment you control for liquidity or price level. Building here means killing most of your own ideas, in public, and treating that as progress rather than embarrassment.

## The hard part is culture, not code

The engineering problems are tractable. The cultural problem is not.

Nepal's market is retail-dominated and young. Information travels through social groups, messaging apps and video channels, where confidence is rewarded and doubt is not. In that environment, the incentive is to publish certainty. A tool that says "this is uncertain" competes against one that says "this will double", and the second is more fun to read.

The bet this project makes is that the appetite for sobriety is larger than it looks — that a growing number of investors have been burned enough times by confident predictions to prefer verified description, and that the ones who prefer it become the market's most durable participants.

That bet may take years to pay off. It is still the right bet, because the alternative is to become one more voice adding noise to a market that already has plenty.

## What success would look like

Not a return figure. Returns are the wrong measure of an information platform, because they are dominated by market direction and by decisions the platform did not make.

Success looks like this:

- An ordinary investor can answer "is this unusual for this stock?" in under a minute, with a number rather than an impression.
- Nobody has to guess which session a figure came from.
- Claims made on the site can be checked, and the failed ones are visible alongside the surviving ones.
- The next generation of Nepali investors treats "what does the data say and how was it measured?" as the normal first question, rather than "what is the tip?"

If systematic thinking becomes ordinary in this market, the project has done its job, whether or not any particular tool is the one that got used.

## Where this goes

The direction is not more prediction. It is more coverage, more validation and more disclosure of method: extending the archive backwards, reading company filings properly instead of skimming them, and being explicit about which parts of the analysis rest on evidence and which rest on judgement.

Nepal's market will get more sophisticated whether or not anyone builds for it. The only question is whether that sophistication arrives as better measurement or as better-marketed guessing. This project exists to push it towards the first.
