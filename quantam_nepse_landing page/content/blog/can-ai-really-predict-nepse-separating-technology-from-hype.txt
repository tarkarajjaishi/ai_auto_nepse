No. Not in the sense the word is normally sold in Nepal — a model that tells you which script will rise tomorrow and by how much. Nothing that exists today does that reliably, and anyone selling it as a finished product is selling something else: a backtest, a lucky run, or a subscription.

That is not the same as saying AI is useless on NEPSE. It is very good at a narrower set of jobs — measuring things consistently, reading far more data than a person can, and refusing to forget an inconvenient result. The gap between those two statements is where most of the hype lives, and closing it is the point of this article.

The honest position is this: a machine can shift the odds slightly and can enforce discipline enormously. It cannot see the future. If you calibrate your expectations to that, the technology becomes genuinely useful. If you do not, you will pay for a black box and blame it when the market does what markets do.

## Three different things get called "prediction"

Most arguments about AI forecasting are people talking past each other, because "predict" hides at least three claims of wildly different difficulty.

1. **Direction.** Will this stock be higher or lower in twenty sessions? This is a coin flip with a small tilt. A model that is right 53 per cent of the time on a large, tradable sample would be a serious result.
2. **Magnitude.** How much will it move? Far harder. Returns in a small market are dominated by a thin tail of large moves, so the average is carried by a handful of events that no model saw coming.
3. **Distribution and risk.** How wide is the range of plausible outcomes, and is volatility rising? This is the one machines actually do well, because volatility clusters and persists in a way that direction does not.

The industry advertises the second, occasionally delivers a weak version of the first, and quietly earns most of its keep on the third. When you read a claim, work out which one is being made before you assess it.

> Volatility is forecastable. Direction is barely forecastable. Price levels are not forecastable. Almost every disappointment with market AI comes from confusing the three.

## Why NEPSE is a hard case in particular

Nepal's market has features that make machine learning harder here than in a large developed exchange, not easier.

- **Short history.** NEPSE is young. A model that wants to learn how the market behaves across several full cycles simply does not have many cycles to learn from. Deep-learning architectures that need hundreds of thousands of independent examples will not find them.
- **A small cross-section.** A few hundred listed companies, many of them highly correlated because banking, development banks, finance and microfinance move together. Your effective sample size is far smaller than your row count suggests.
- **Retail dominance and thin liquidity.** A signal that only works on a stock trading a few thousand units a day is not a signal you can act on. Backtests that ignore liquidity produce beautiful equity curves that cannot be executed.
- **Structural breaks.** Regulation, interest-rate cycles and market plumbing change. A model fitted to one regime can be confidently wrong in the next, and it will not tell you it has gone stale.

None of this makes quantitative work pointless. It means the honest expectations are modest and the testing has to be stricter, not looser, than in a bigger market.

## The failure that produces most false claims

The single most common way a NEPSE "AI prediction" turns out to be nothing is multiple testing. Try enough indicators, thresholds and holding periods on the same history and something will look excellent purely by chance. With a few hundred symbols and a decade or so of daily bars, a determined researcher can manufacture an impressive result in an afternoon.

The defences are unglamorous and they are the whole job.

- **Hold out data you never look at** until the strategy is final. Not a random split — a later time period, because time is what actually passes.
- **Report every year separately.** A strategy that made all its money in one exceptional year is a story about that year, not a rule about the market.
- **Count the tests.** If you tried forty variants, the best of forty needs a much higher bar than a single pre-registered idea.
- **Compare against a fair null.** Against random entry into the same universe at the same time, not against cash.
- **Include costs and slippage.** Commission and the spread you actually cross, at the size you actually trade.

Most published market strategies do not survive this treatment. That is a fact about strategies, not a failure of nerve.

## Where AI genuinely earns its place on NEPSE

Strip out the forecasting fantasy and a substantial amount of real value remains.

**Reading the floorsheet at scale.** NEPSE publishes broker-level trade data, which is unusually granular compared with many exchanges and badly under-used by retail investors. No human reads months of it across every symbol. A machine does it in seconds, and can measure concentration, churn and flow consistently. Interpreting those measures still needs care — see the conservation warning below — but the measurement itself is a genuine capability gain.

**Consistency of judgement.** A model applies the same definition of "unusual volume" to every stock on every day. It does not get bored on the four hundredth symbol and it does not let yesterday's loss colour today's reading.

**Triage.** Turning a few hundred companies into a shortlist of a dozen worth your attention is enormously valuable even if the ranking inside that dozen is noise.

**Language work.** Extracting figures from quarterly reports, summarising disclosures, and flagging changes in tone between one report and the next. This is where large language models are strongest, provided every extracted number stays traceable to its source document.

**Risk and sizing.** Once you hold a view, software applies position limits identically every time. This is the least exciting item on the list and probably the most profitable.

## The identity that kills a whole family of claims

Be sceptical of any product claiming to detect a hidden net buyer accumulating across the market. Every share bought is sold. Market-wide net buy and sell imbalance is identically zero by construction — not approximately zero, exactly zero. A metric that appears to show otherwise is almost always re-describing turnover, or has an aggregation error in it.

The same caution applies at the single-stock level. "A broker is accumulating this stock" is true of some broker on virtually every symbol on virtually every day, because someone must be the largest net buyer. The claim only means something when it is measured against what is normal for that stock and that day, and when it has been tested out of sample against what happened next.

## How to interrogate a prediction claim

Before you trust a number to make a decision, ask for these. If the answers do not exist, you have a marketing asset, not a model.

1. What exactly is being predicted, over what horizon, and on which universe of stocks?
2. What is the out-of-sample period, and was it truly untouched during development?
3. What does year-by-year performance look like, including the bad years?
4. What is the comparison — random entry into the same universe, or a flattering benchmark?
5. Are transaction costs and realistic liquidity included?
6. How many variants were tried before this one was chosen?
7. What would make this model wrong, and how would you know it had stopped working?

The seventh question is the one that separates researchers from salespeople. A real quantitative process has a documented graveyard of ideas it killed. If you are shown only successes, you are being shown a selected sample.

## What to actually do with this

Use machine analysis for the jobs it is good at and keep the decision yours.

- Let a model narrow the universe and measure activity, then do your own reading on the shortlist. [How to analyze a NEPSE stock before buying](/blogs/how-to-analyze-a-nepse-stock-before-buying) covers what that reading should contain.
- Treat any signal without a documented out-of-sample test as an observation, not evidence. If you want to test one yourself, start with [backtesting a NEPSE trading strategy](/blogs/backtesting-a-nepse-trading-strategy-complete-beginner-guide).
- Prefer tools that show their working — the inputs, the definition, the history — over tools that show only a score.
- Size positions as though the model is right slightly more often than chance, because that is the realistic ceiling.
