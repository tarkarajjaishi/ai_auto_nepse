There are hundreds of published technical indicators. There are roughly five things they measure. Almost every indicator is one of those five measurements with a different smoothing constant, a different normalisation, or a different name attached by whoever popularised it, and treating them as independent inputs is the most common way a model's feature set gets ruined.

For an automated system this matters more than it does for a human chart reader. A person glancing at three overlapping momentum oscillators simply sees the same thing three times and moves on. A model given the same three inputs weights that single piece of information three times over, and then reports high confidence in a view that rests on one measurement.

So the useful question is not which indicators to use. It is which distinct quantities exist, which single representative of each to feed the model, and what each one breaks on.

## The five things indicators actually measure

1. **Trend or location.** Where price sits relative to its own recent history. Moving averages, the distance between price and a moving average, and channel positions all answer this.
2. **Momentum or rate of change.** How quickly price has moved recently, usually rescaled into a bounded range. RSI, stochastic oscillators and rate-of-change all belong here, and they are heavily correlated with each other.
3. **Volatility.** How wide the typical move is. Average true range, the standard deviation of returns, and the width of a Bollinger band are three views of the same underlying quantity.
4. **Participation.** How much trading is happening relative to normal, and where in the range it occurred. Volume ratios, on-balance volume and volume-weighted average price live here.
5. **Relative strength.** How the instrument is doing compared with its sector or the market rather than in absolute terms.

If your feature set does not have at least one input from each of those five, it has a blind spot. If it has six inputs from any single one, it has redundancy that will be invisible in the code and obvious in the results.

> Two features called different things can be the same number. Compare their values, not their names, before you trust a weighting.

## What each family genuinely tells a model

**Moving averages** are a low-pass filter and nothing more. Their value is that they define a reference level that adapts, so "expensive" and "cheap" become relative to the stock's own recent behaviour rather than to an absolute number. Their cost is lag, which is not a flaw to be engineered away but the price of the smoothing. Longer window, more stability, more delay.

**MACD** is the difference between two moving averages, so it is a trend measurement expressed as a rate. Understand that it is arithmetic on the same input as the moving averages you already have.

**RSI and stochastic oscillators** compress recent movement into a bounded range, conventionally with readings above seventy and below thirty called overbought and oversold. The bounded scale is genuinely useful for a model, because it makes stocks comparable regardless of price. The overbought and oversold labels are much weaker than their reputation: a strongly trending stock can stay at an extreme reading for a long stretch, and treating that as a reversal signal is how people fight trends.

**Average true range** is the most under-used input in retail practice and one of the most valuable for a model. It converts every threshold you care about — stop distance, what counts as a large move, position size — into units of the stock's own volatility. A fixed percentage stop applied across a whole universe is really a bet on which stocks are volatile.

**ADX** measures how much trend exists without saying which direction, which makes it a regime input rather than a signal. Its main use is deciding which other rules should be active.

**Volume measures** are the noisiest family and the most valuable when handled carefully. The single most important transformation is to compare a stock's volume with its own typical range rather than with the market's, because absolute turnover across a market is enormously uneven.

**Relative strength against a sector index** is frequently the highest-value feature in a cross-sectional model. NEPSE publishes sub-indices for banking, development banks, finance, hydropower, microfinance, life and non-life insurance, hotels and tourism, manufacturing, trading, investment, mutual funds and others. A large part of any stock's movement is its sector's movement, and removing that leaves a much cleaner measure of the stock itself.

## The NEPSE-specific complications

Indicators are defined on assumptions that a market like Nepal's does not always satisfy.

- **Daily price limits truncate the range.** When a stock reaches its permitted daily limit, the recorded high and low understate the actual pressure, and range-based indicators read low precisely when the market is most one-sided.
- **Thin trading breaks smoothing.** A stock that trades sparsely produces flat stretches punctuated by jumps. Every indicator computed on that series inherits the artefact. Screen for a minimum liquidity level before computing anything, rather than trusting the numbers and being surprised later.
- **Corporate actions poison history.** Bonus and rights adjustments restate the price series. Every indicator with a lookback window that spans the restatement is wrong until the series is adjusted, and the failure is silent.
- **Correlation across the market is high.** When most stocks move together, an indicator that looks like it is selecting stocks may only be reflecting the day. Demeaning by date across the universe is the fix.
- **The floorsheet adds a family the standard toolkit lacks.** Broker-level data supports participation measures that no conventional indicator captures — concentration of a session across brokers, net flow by broker over a window, how broad participation is compared with the stock's own baseline. Treat these with the same scepticism as anything else: many of them correlate strongly with plain turnover, and some share-count-based measures are an inverse-price proxy in disguise. Check what survives after neutralising volume and price.

## Parameters are where overfitting hides

Every indicator has a lookback length, and every lookback length is a free parameter. Search across a few indicators and a few window lengths and you will find a combination that performed beautifully on your sample. That is arithmetic, not discovery — with enough combinations, something always fits.

Three habits keep this under control.

- Choose parameters for a reason and leave them alone. Round numbers chosen in advance are more honest than tuned ones.
- Check the neighbourhood. If a fourteen-session window works and a twelve or sixteen-session window does not, you have found noise. A real effect degrades gradually as you move the parameter.
- Prefer fewer indicators with fixed settings over many with tuned ones. The combinatorics of the second option guarantee a flattering backtest.

## A defensible minimum feature set

If you were building a NEPSE model and wanted the smallest set covering all five measurements without redundancy, this is a reasonable starting point.

1. Price relative to a medium-length moving average, expressed in units of that stock's volatility.
2. Realised volatility or average true range over a recent window, and its ratio to a longer window so the model can see whether volatility is rising.
3. One bounded momentum oscillator, chosen and never supplemented with a second.
4. Turnover relative to the stock's own median turnover over a longer window.
5. Return relative to the stock's sector index over the same horizon you are predicting.
6. Optionally, one floorsheet participation measure that has survived a turnover control.

Six inputs. That is not a limitation; a compact, non-redundant set with a clean chronological validation will beat a sprawling one nearly every time, and when it fails you will be able to see why.

## What to check before you trust an indicator

- Compute the correlation matrix of your features. Anything above roughly nine-tenths is a duplicate — keep one.
- Confirm every indicator uses only data available at the time, with no centred windows and no full-sample statistics.
- Verify behaviour on illiquid names and on days at the price limit.
- Confirm the series is corporate-action adjusted across every lookback window used.
- Test whether the indicator still carries information after removing the market-wide daily move.
- Report performance per year, not just in aggregate.

Indicators are a language for describing price behaviour compactly and comparably. That is all they are, and it is enough. They do not contain a forecast, and any system that presents them as one is selling the wrong thing.
