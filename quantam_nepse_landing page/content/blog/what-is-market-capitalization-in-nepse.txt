Market capitalisation is the market's price for an entire company: the last traded price multiplied by the number of listed shares. A company with ten million shares whose last trade was at three hundred rupees has a market capitalisation of three billion rupees. That is the whole calculation.

What people do with the number is where the trouble starts. Market capitalisation gets treated as a measure of size, of quality, of how much money is in a stock, and of whether a company is cheap. It is only reliably the first of those, and even that comes with conditions specific to NEPSE that most retail screens never mention.

## What the number actually measures

It measures what the marginal buyer and seller agreed on, extended to every share in existence. That extension is the assumption worth examining, because the last trade may have been a few units between two people while the overwhelming majority of shares never came near the market.

> Market capitalisation measures what the market thinks a company is worth, not what anyone has actually put into it.

Nobody has invested the market capitalisation into the company. Money paid for shares in the secondary market goes to the seller, not to the business. A company whose market value rises by a billion rupees has received nothing at all from that rise.

## Total capitalisation versus free float

This is the single most important adjustment in NEPSE, and it is why the exchange publishes a float-based index alongside the main one.

A large portion of many listed Nepali companies is held by promoters and is not freely tradable. Government and institutional holdings add to the locked portion. The result is that **total market capitalisation and the value of shares actually available to trade are different numbers**, sometimes by a wide margin.

The practical consequences:

- A company can look large by total capitalisation while its tradable float is small enough that ordinary retail flows move the price sharply.
- Index weights based on total capitalisation overstate the influence of companies with big locked blocks, which is precisely the distortion a float index exists to correct.
- Comparing two companies of similar total capitalisation tells you very little if one has most of its shares locked and the other does not.

Before using capitalisation for anything, check the promoter and public split. It is disclosed, and it changes the interpretation completely.

## Why a capitalisation series over time is not like-for-like

Nepali companies issue bonus shares and right shares regularly, and both change the share count.

- A **bonus issue** raises the share count while the price adjusts down on the ex-date. In theory capitalisation is unchanged. You own more shares of the same company and nothing was created.
- A **right issue** raises the share count and brings cash into the company. Capitalisation rises partly because the business is genuinely larger, not because the market revalued what already existed.

So a company whose market capitalisation doubled over several years may have got there through issuance rather than appreciation. When comparing a company with its own past, use per-share figures alongside capitalisation, and remember that per-share history itself needs adjusting for bonus issues before it means anything.

## Sector concentration and what it does to market-wide numbers

NEPSE's total capitalisation is dominated by financial institutions. Commercial banks alone carry heavy weight, and the broader financial group, including development banks, finance companies, microfinance and insurance, accounts for a large share of listed value.

That matters whenever you read market commentary.

- "The market is up" frequently means the banking group is up. A broad index in a concentrated market behaves closer to a sector index than most people realise.
- A sector that dominates the news, hydropower being the obvious example, can hold a modest share of total capitalisation relative to its share of attention and trading activity.
- Comparing sub-index moves without knowing each sector's weight produces confident, wrong conclusions about market breadth. The structure is unpacked in [understanding NEPSE sub-indices](/blogs/understanding-nepse-sub-indices-banking-hydropower-microfinance-and-more).

## What market capitalisation does not tell you

- **Not value.** Cheap and expensive are ratios. Capitalisation is a level. A large company can be cheap and a small one wildly expensive.
- **Not quality.** Size reflects price and share count, and both can be inflated by things unrelated to how well the business runs.
- **Not liquidity.** This one surprises people. A large capitalisation with a small float can be harder to exit than a smaller company whose shares are widely distributed. Check turnover directly.
- **Not stability.** A large capitalisation built on a high multiple is not defensive. It simply has further to fall.
- **Not money invested.** Worth repeating, because the phrase about billions being wiped off the market implies a transfer that never happened. Prices changed. Nobody collected the difference except those who actually traded.

Because the price input is the last trade, every weakness of that number is inherited here, including staleness on thin stocks. That is covered in [what LTP means](/blogs/what-is-ltp-in-nepse-complete-explanation).

## Using it sensibly

Market capitalisation earns its place in three jobs.

1. **Bucketing the universe by size.** Grouping stocks into large, medium and small lets you compare like with like, and it exposes screen results that are secretly dominated by tiny companies.
2. **Understanding index behaviour.** Once you know which companies carry weight, index moves stop being mysterious and start being arithmetic.
3. **Sanity-checking a valuation.** Divide capitalisation by earnings and you have the [P/E ratio](/blogs/what-is-p-e-ratio-and-how-does-it). Divide by book equity and you have price to book. Both are more informative than capitalisation alone.

A useful discipline: whenever you note a company's capitalisation, write two numbers beside it, the free float percentage and the average daily turnover. Together those three describe size, availability and tradability. Any one of them alone misleads.

## One ratio worth building yourself

Divide a company's average daily turnover by its market capitalisation and you get a rough measure of how much of the company changes hands in a normal session. It is crude, and it is far more informative than either number on its own.

A company with a large capitalisation and a very low ratio is one where the market rarely prices the whole business. Its quoted value rests on a thin stream of trades, so the price can move a long way on flows that would barely register elsewhere. A company with a high ratio is being actively repriced every day, which is not automatically better, but it does mean the quoted number reflects something more than a handful of transactions.

Track the ratio for a stock over several months and you learn its normal range. Once you know that range, a sudden change in it is one of the few genuinely informative things a small market gives you, and it costs nothing to compute.

## What to watch

- Check whether a published figure is **total or float-adjusted** before comparing anything to anything.
- Restate a **capitalisation series in per-share terms** before concluding a company has grown. Issuance is not appreciation.
- Treat **market-wide capitalisation changes** as price changes, not money flows. Every share bought was sold, so nothing accumulated anywhere.
- Never infer **liquidity** from size. Verify it with turnover and number of trades.
- Watch **weight, not headline moves**, when deciding which sectors actually drove a session.

Market capitalisation is a well-defined number answering a narrow question: what every share would cost at the last traded price. Kept to that question it is useful. Stretched into a measure of worth, safety or invested money, it becomes one of the most confidently misused figures on any NEPSE screen.
