The case for automated market intelligence in Nepal has nothing to do with AI being impressive. It rests on a specific structural fact: NEPSE publishes more data than the country has research capacity to process, and the gap between the two is filled by rumour.

That is a solvable problem, and it is solvable cheaply, because processing capacity is the one input that has become dramatically less expensive in the last few years while analyst time has not.

The argument below is deliberately unromantic. It is not that machines will outsmart the market. It is that a market with a small professional research base and an unusually rich public dataset has an obvious, under-exploited opportunity, and the tools to take it are now within reach of anyone willing to do the unglamorous work.

## The deficit is research capacity, not data

Count what is publicly available for the Nepal Stock Exchange: daily bars for every listed company across every sector sub-index, quarterly reports, corporate action and auction notices, and a broker-level floorsheet of executed trades. That is a substantial public record.

Now count who processes it thoroughly. Independent, published, method-disclosed research on Nepali listed companies is scarce. Most listings receive no continuous analytical coverage at all. A retail investor looking for a considered view on a mid-sized company will often find nothing that discloses its method, and will fall back on a headline number or someone's opinion.

The mismatch is the whole story. Data is abundant, attention is scarce, and attention does not scale with effort. One analyst covering thirty companies well is a good analyst. The market has several hundred listings.

## Why thin coverage matters more in a small market

In a deeply researched market, public information is absorbed into prices quickly because thousands of people are competing to do exactly that. The marginal value of reading one more filing is low.

In a market where coverage is thin, that is not true. Ordinary public data — a quarterly result, an unusual pattern of participation, a change in a company's disclosure language — can sit unexamined simply because nobody had the time. The advantage available is not exotic. It comes from doing the ordinary work completely rather than partially.

> In a thinly covered market, thoroughness is the edge. That is precisely the kind of edge software is good at and humans are not.

This also explains why the payoff from automation is larger here than in a mature market. The task being automated is not beating a room full of quantitative funds. It is reading everything, consistently, when the current alternative is reading a fraction of it.

## The floorsheet is an asset nobody is spending

Nepal's exchange publishes broker-level trade data. Many larger exchanges do not release trade-level broker identifiers publicly at all. It is the single richest dataset available to a Nepali retail investor and it goes almost entirely unused, because reading it by hand is impractical.

Processed at scale, it answers descriptive questions that a traditional workflow cannot even pose. Was a session's volume concentrated in a few brokers or spread across many? Did participation broaden or narrow as the price moved? How does a broker's recent net position in a stock compare with its own typical behaviour?

These are statements about what happened, not forecasts. That distinction is the reason they are trustworthy: describing the past accurately is a much lower bar than predicting the future, and it is a bar that can actually be cleared.

The dataset also enforces humility on anyone who works with it seriously. Every share bought is sold, so any net buy or sell imbalance measured across the whole market is exactly zero by construction. A large family of exciting-sounding metrics turns out to be turnover in disguise, and they collapse the moment you control for liquidity or price. Anyone publishing floorsheet analysis without having killed most of their own ideas first has not tested them.

## What rumour fills in

Where verified analysis is absent, something replaces it. In Nepal that something is social: messaging groups, video channels, and confident commentary where outcomes are never audited. The structural problem with this channel is not dishonesty, it is selection. Wins are shared and losses are silent, so the base rate an observer infers from their feed is far better than reality.

That distortion is expensive. It pushes participation towards frequent trading, towards stories rather than statements, and towards whichever narrative is currently loudest. A market whose retail base learns its habits from a filtered sample of successes will keep repeating the same cycle.

Automated, transparent measurement will not eliminate rumour. It does give a sceptical investor something to check a rumour against in under a minute, which is a meaningful change.

## What automation genuinely fixes

- **Coverage.** Every listed symbol screened every session against the same stated criteria, rather than the handful anyone has time for.
- **Consistency.** The same rule applied the day after a loss as the day after a gain. Discipline becomes a property of the process rather than of the person.
- **Memory.** Today compared against a full archive rather than against recollection, which is reconstructed and flatters whatever you already believe.
- **Reading at scale.** Language models can extract figures from hundreds of filings and flag what changed since the last one, with every figure pointing back to its source line so a human can verify the ones that matter.
- **Falsifiability.** Rules stated precisely enough to be tested against history — and, crucially, to be shown not to work.

## What it does not fix

Honesty about the limits is what makes the rest credible.

- **It does not predict prices.** Short-horizon price movement is dominated by noise in every liquid market studied properly, and NEPSE is not exempt.
- **It does not remove risk.** It relocates it. A system that sizes positions mechanically can lose mechanically. What good software does is make risk explicit and bounded, not absent.
- **It does not settle valuation.** Whether a price is justified depends on assumptions about growth, credit quality and policy that no dataset resolves.
- **It does not survive regime change gracefully.** Models learn from history. When rules or market structure shift, history is a weaker guide, and confidence is usually highest at exactly that moment.
- **It does not fix thin liquidity.** A signal you cannot act on without moving the price is not an opportunity.

## The risk of getting this wrong

There is a version of this that makes the market worse, and it is worth naming.

**False precision.** A number carried to two decimals implies a confidence that the underlying data does not support. Precision is not accuracy, and displaying the first while lacking the second is a form of misinformation.

**Black boxes.** A score you cannot decompose asks for faith. If a platform cannot explain a figure in a sentence you can check by hand, it has not made you better informed — it has moved the source of your tips from a group chat to a dashboard.

**Backtest theatre.** Any strategy tuned on the same history it is measured against will look excellent. Without out-of-sample evidence, per-year results and a comparison against random entry, a performance claim is decoration.

**Crowding.** If enough participants act on the same widely available signal in a market with limited depth, the signal degrades and the crowd's exit is not orderly.

## What good looks like

A short checklist, useful whether you are building a tool or choosing one:

1. Every figure states which session it came from, and the page warns when it is behind the market.
2. Live and historical data are visibly distinct during trading hours, and stale values render as stale rather than as prices.
3. Description and prediction are separated in the language, not blurred together.
4. Methods are disclosed well enough that a competent reader could reproduce them.
5. Failures are published alongside successes.
6. Uncertainty is shown rather than smoothed away.

## The case, in one line

Nepal does not need artificial intelligence to guess where the index is going. It needs the boring, complete, repeatable processing of data that is already public, delivered in a form an ordinary investor can check — and it needs whoever provides that to be honest about how little anyone can know about tomorrow.

If you want the market to become more sophisticated, the practical starting point is not a better forecast. It is a higher standard for what counts as evidence.
