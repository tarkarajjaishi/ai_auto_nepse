The future of NEPSE analysis will not arrive as a single product. It will arrive as a gradual redistribution of who can afford to do serious work. Techniques that until recently required a research desk — processing every trade in the market, testing a hypothesis across years of history, reading hundreds of disclosures — are becoming things one person with a laptop can do. That is the actual change, and it is structural rather than technological.

What it will not produce is a machine that knows where the index is going. Prediction is not the frontier. The frontier is coverage: the fact that most listed Nepali securities have never been analysed carefully by anybody, and that the public data needed to do it has been sitting unread.

This article looks at what changes for each group in the market, what has to improve before the change is worth much, and what stays exactly as it is.

## The shift is from scarcity of analysis to scarcity of judgement

For most of NEPSE's history, analysis was the bottleneck. There were few analysts, thin coverage, and the effort of examining a company properly meant most companies went unexamined. Investors filled the gap with tips, sentiment and familiarity.

When machines do the examining, analysis stops being scarce. What becomes scarce is the ability to decide what to do with it — which findings matter, which are artefacts, which are true but irrelevant to your horizon. That is a different skill from the one the market currently rewards, and it does not become obsolete when the software improves. It becomes more valuable, because there is more output to sift.

> Cheap analysis does not make judgement less important. It makes bad judgement more expensive, because you can now act on more things.

## What changes for the retail investor

The most concrete change is that the ordinary work becomes complete rather than partial.

- **The whole market gets screened, not the part you remember.** Retail attention in Nepal concentrates in a handful of familiar names. A machine applies the identical test to every listed security, so the short list is produced by the rule rather than by recall.
- **The floorsheet becomes readable.** NEPSE's broker-level trade data is unusually granular and almost entirely unused, because a single session's file is beyond manual reading. Software makes it ordinary.
- **Comparisons become fair.** Each stock gets measured against its own history rather than against a threshold borrowed from a foreign textbook. A volume level that is dramatic for one security is a quiet day for another.
- **Claims become checkable.** When a rule is written down, you can test whether it ever worked. Most tips cannot be tested at all, which is precisely their appeal.

The change that will not happen is the one most retail investors are hoping for. Nothing here tells you what a stock will do next week.

## What changes for brokers and advisers

Brokers hold operational data and client relationships that no external tool has. The likely direction is that routine research becomes commoditised — anybody can generate a competent factsheet on any listed company — while the advisory role sharpens around things software cannot do: understanding a client's actual circumstances, holding them to a plan during a drawdown, and saying no.

Advisers who compete on information will find that competition harder. Advisers who compete on behaviour will find it easier, because the same tools that inform clients also make clients more confident, and overconfidence is the expensive part of retail investing.

## What changes for analysts

Less than the headlines suggest, but in an uncomfortable direction. The parts of an analyst's job that are transcription, spreadsheet maintenance and ratio computation are already automatable. The parts that involve reconciling a company's numbers with what is actually happening in its business, questioning management's framing, and noticing what has been left out of a disclosure are not.

An analyst whose output is a summary of a report is competing directly with software that summarises reports. An analyst whose output is a judgement about whether the report is honest is not. ## What has to improve before any of this matters

The constraint on quantitative work in Nepal is data quality, not model sophistication. Several things need to be true before better maths adds anything.

1. **Machine-readable disclosures.** Financial reports published as scanned images are effectively unavailable to computation, whatever the model. Structured, consistent filings are worth more than any algorithm.
2. **Clean corporate-action history.** Bonus issues, rights and dividends shift a price series without anything happening to the business. An unadjusted series produces phantom falls that models faithfully learn from. This is the most common silent defect in Nepali market datasets.
3. **Complete and consistent historical files.** Older archived data often differs in format from recent data — different line endings, different number formats, different broker codes. A parser that silently drops the rows it cannot read will produce a beautifully clean result from a fraction of the data, and nothing will warn you.
4. **Timestamped data everywhere.** A number without its session is unusable for any test, because you cannot tell whether the information was available when the decision was supposedly made.
5. **Enough history per security.** Many listed instruments simply do not have enough trading history for a statistical claim about them to mean anything. Honest systems say so rather than producing a confident score.

None of these are exciting. All of them determine whether the analysis on top is real.

## The traps that will produce most of the bad output

As more AI tools appear in the Nepali market, expect these specific failures, because they are the ones that survive casual inspection.

**Look-ahead contamination.** A test that uses information which did not exist at the time of the simulated decision will always look brilliant. It is easy to introduce accidentally — a stock list built from today's listed universe already excludes everything that was delisted, which flatters every historical result.

**Overfitting sold as discovery.** Try enough variants and one will look excellent by chance. The honest test is whether it survives on data the designer had never seen, and whether it works in most years rather than one.

**Restated metrics.** A great many broker-level metrics turn out to be turnover under another name, and many share-count metrics are an inverse price proxy. If a signal correlates almost exactly with something trivial, it is that trivial thing.

**Arithmetic mistaken for insight.** Every share bought is sold, so any market-wide net buying imbalance is zero by construction. Any product claiming to detect a hidden net buyer across the whole market has found a bug, not a whale.

## What does not change

Three things, and they are the ones that determine most outcomes.

- **Ownership risk.** Holding a share means holding a business's fortunes. No amount of processing changes what happens if the business deteriorates.
- **Behaviour.** The gap between a strategy's results and an investor's results is caused by the investor. Better information does not close it and sometimes widens it.
- **Uncertainty.** Markets are not solvable. A system that always produces a confident answer has stopped measuring and started performing.

## What to watch for as this develops

If you want to track whether the shift in Nepal is real or cosmetic, watch the infrastructure rather than the marketing.

- Are disclosures becoming structured and machine-readable, or still published as images?
- Do tools state which session each number came from, and behave visibly differently when data is stale?
- Do the people building them publish their failures — the strategies that did not survive testing — or only their successes?
- Does anything on offer separate description from prediction, or is everything presented as a signal?

The market that gets built on honest answers to those questions will be genuinely better than the one we have. The one built on backtests and confidence will look identical from the outside and cost people money.
