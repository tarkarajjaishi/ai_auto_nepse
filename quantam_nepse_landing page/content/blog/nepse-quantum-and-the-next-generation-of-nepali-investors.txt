A new kind of investor has arrived in Nepal's market. They opened their demat account online rather than at a counter. They place orders from a phone. They learned what a P/E ratio is from a video, not a textbook, and they discuss positions in group chats rather than at a broker's office.

This changes the market in ways that are easy to describe badly. The lazy version says young investors are speculative and undisciplined. The more accurate version is that they inherited an information environment that rewards confidence over accuracy, and almost nobody taught them how to check a claim.

That is a fixable problem, and it is the more interesting one. This article is about what this cohort is genuinely good at, what the environment costs them, and the specific habits that separate investors who are still here in ten years from those who are not.

## What is actually different about this cohort

Four traits, and they cut both ways.

- **Mobile-first and impatient with friction.** They will use a tool that answers a question in ten seconds and abandon one that takes ten minutes. That raises the bar for anyone building for them, which is good.
- **Social learning.** Knowledge arrives through networks rather than institutions. Good ideas spread fast; so do bad ones, and the bad ones travel faster because they are more exciting.
- **Comfortable with software.** Screeners, charts, spreadsheets and dashboards are not intimidating. A generation ago, explaining a percentile was the hard part. Now the hard part is explaining why the percentile matters.
- **Sceptical of authority, credulous about peers.** They will question a bank's research and accept a stranger's screenshot. The scepticism is healthy; it is just aimed in the wrong direction.

None of that is unique to Nepal. What is distinctive here is the combination of a retail-dominated market, a young exchange with a limited public research culture, and unusually granular public data that almost nobody uses.

## The information environment they inherited

Consider what a new investor actually encounters. Group chats where positions are announced but never closed out publicly. Videos where a confident presenter explains why a stock will move, with no record of the last ten predictions. Testimonials from people who did well, which is a selection effect rather than evidence — the ones who lost are simply quieter.

The result is a systematic distortion. The visible evidence is overwhelmingly positive because losses are private, so the base rate a beginner infers from their feed is far better than the real one.

> The reason tips feel more useful than data is that failed tips leave no trace and failed data leaves a chart.

Add to this the natural bias of anyone who entered during a rising phase: everything worked, so everything they did must have been correct. Method and luck are indistinguishable in a bull market, which is exactly when the most people are forming habits.

## What this generation is unusually well placed to do

The pessimistic story is wrong on the most important point. This cohort has a real advantage: they are the first group of Nepali investors for whom systematic analysis is technically accessible.

The floorsheet NEPSE publishes is broker-level trade data of a kind most retail investors in the world are never handed. It has been sitting there, unread, because processing it required tools and patience. That barrier has collapsed. A curious investor today can ask, and answer, questions that were previously the exclusive province of institutions with a research desk:

- Is today's turnover unusual for this stock, measured against its own history rather than the market's?
- Was the session's volume concentrated in a handful of brokers or spread widely?
- Has participation broadened as the price rose, or has it thinned?

That is a genuine shift in who can do serious work, and it has arrived at the same time as a generation with the technical comfort to take advantage of it.

## Three habits that separate the ones who last

1. **Define what you are looking for before you look.** Write down the criteria first. If you decide after seeing the chart, you will always find a justification — your brain is extremely good at supplying one, and it will feel like analysis.
2. **Measure against the stock's own history.** "Volume is high" is meaningless without a reference. High compared with last week, or with the past two years? A rupee threshold that suits a large bank tells you nothing about a small hydropower company. Every comparison should be relative to the instrument itself.
3. **Write the reasoning down at the time of the decision.** Not the outcome — the reasoning. Six months later this is the only way to tell a good decision from a lucky one, and telling them apart is the entire mechanism by which anyone improves.

The third habit is the one everybody skips and the only one that compounds. An investor with a written record of fifty decisions knows things about themselves that no course can teach: which sectors they consistently misjudge, whether they cut losses late, whether their conviction correlates with anything at all.

## What tools owe this generation

If software is going to be the main teacher, it carries an obligation. A platform aimed at newer investors should:

- **Show which session every number came from**, and warn when a pre-computed board is behind the market. A stale figure presented as current teaches people to trust something they should not.
- **Separate description from prediction in its language.** Facts and forecasts must not share a typeface.
- **Teach the definition alongside the number.** A percentile with no explanation is a magic number; a percentile with one sentence of definition is a transferable skill.
- **Show uncertainty rather than hiding it.** A system with a confident answer for everything is not measuring anything.
- **Refuse to gamify.** Streaks, leaderboards, celebratory animations on a profitable trade — every one of these trains frequency, and frequency is the enemy of returns for a retail investor. Attention and outcomes are different objectives, and optimising for the first damages the second.

That last point deserves emphasis, because the design patterns that make an app addictive are well known and cheap to copy. A platform serving investors should be measurably boring at exactly the moments a game would be exciting.

## A realistic learning path

If you are early in this and want a sequence rather than a firehose, this order works.

1. **Mechanics first.** How orders match, what settlement means, what the trading session looks like, what your costs actually are. Unglamorous and non-optional.
2. **The vocabulary.** LTP, market capitalisation, EPS, book value and the multiples built on them. Learn what each one hides as well as what it shows.
3. **Reading a financial report.** Not the headline profit — the quality of it. Where earnings came from, whether they repeat, what the balance sheet says about risk.
4. **Comparative measurement.** Percentiles, ranges and normalisation. This is the step that turns data into judgement, and it is the one most people skip straight past.
5. **Testing your own ideas.** How to check whether a rule would have worked historically, and why an in-sample result proves almost nothing.
6. **Risk and sizing.** Last in the list, first in importance. How much you lose when you are wrong determines your outcome far more than how often you are right.

## What to do this week

Pick one stock you already own or follow. Write down, before you look at anything, what would make you buy more and what would make you sell. Then find the numbers: where today's turnover sits in that stock's own range, what the last quarterly report actually said, how concentrated the recent trading has been.

You will probably find that your written criteria were vaguer than they felt, and that the data neither confirms nor refutes them. That is not a failure of the exercise. That is the exercise. The gap between "I have a view" and "I have a testable view" is where the next generation of Nepali investors will either build a durable edge or spend a decade repeating the same mistake with more confidence each time.
